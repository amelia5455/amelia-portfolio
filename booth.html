Overview

Booth is an analog photobooth finder I designed and built on my own, from a single-file web prototype to a SwiftUI app on the App Store track. It is a paper-and-ink map of real machines, placed by hand, close to 400 of them across 11 cities. Every strip you shoot gets a private library to live in: no feed, no followers, passwordless sign-in. I owned all of it: the design system, the iOS app, the backend, and the pipeline that ships it.

Background

Analog, in a digital world

Photobooths are having a resurgence. In a feed full of disposable images, a printed strip is tangible, a little imperfect, and yours. People seek them out at bars, arcades, malls, and events, and the strip usually ends up on a fridge or a mirror. The ritual is the point, and it is exactly the kind of small, real-world delight I like designing around.

Problem

Easy to love, hard to find

The catch is finding one. Booths appear and disappear, live in places you would never think to check, and rarely show up reliably in a map search. You usually only remember you want a strip when you are already out, with no easy way to know where the nearest booth is, or whether it still exists.

North star

How might we make finding, remembering, and collecting photobooths as effortless as taking the picture?

Approach

One source of truth, ported carefully

I built Booth the way I wanted it to behave: one design that two surfaces obey, in a single material, held honest by the real device.

The prototype is law

I built a single HTML file first and made it the design source of truth. The iOS app is a port of that prototype, verified pixel by pixel and motion by motion on a real phone. Every disagreement between the two is a bug in the app, not a fork in the design, and both surfaces get the same change or neither does. That one rule kept the app from quietly drifting away from the thing I actually designed.

Paper and ink, as a system

The whole app is built from one material: a paper ground, an ink foreground, one serif for names and one mono for everything utilitarian. Even the map obeys. The basemap tiles are pushed through a desaturated, low-contrast, sepia wash so the map reads as a printed page rather than a tech product, with the attribution folded behind an (i).

Design

The decisions that make it Booth

A handful of small, opinionated calls did most of the work of making the app feel like a hand-made object rather than a utility.

From social to private

The first build had a social tab: likes, comments, a feed. I deleted it, not deferred it. It became Strips, a private wall of the booths you have visited, three columns, every print on a slim paper mat at a uniform 3:4 crop. A staggered salon layout got rejected twice, because uniform prints should hang level.

Pins, then the swap

Pins are the booth’s own drawing, tinted by status. My first scheme darkened saved booths to full ink. I reversed it after living with it: the unsaved field is ink and loud, and saving quiets a booth to stone. You dim what is already yours; the undiscovered stays loud.

Save is a bookmark, not a heart

Saving a booth is a bookmark, not a like. The button inverts its own circle, an ink disc with a paper mark, rather than swapping the icon. Small, but it keeps saving from reading as social approval.

One color belongs to the user

Each person picks a booth color that becomes their avatar and identity, and never touches the app’s chrome. It is the one pigment that is theirs. I later pulled it off the map pins too, so a user’s color stays about them, not about navigation.

Trust is hand-made

Nothing lands on the map unmoderated. Adding a booth needs a photo as proof and queues for review. Location permission is optional and never leaves the phone, with a city picker for anyone who declines. Outside 80km of any city, the app says “Not here yet.” instead of faking an empty map.

Accessibility is a setting

The accessibility screen has a Simplified Language toggle that swaps every wry flavor line for a plain sentence, and a typeface switcher (Default, OpenDyslexic, Lexend) where each button is set in its own face. Supporting OpenDyslexic meant rewriting its vertical metrics so switching typefaces moves no edges.

Motion has a grammar

Movement in Booth follows one rulebook. The map is the floor; Strips and You slide up over it; onboarding steps push in from the right. Entering the app does not load a new screen, it lifts a veil off a map that was already alive underneath. The app intro even plays the booth’s own story: the slot pops, a strip drops with a wobble, the camera dives into the middle frame, and you land standing inside it. Reduce Motion sees none of it, by design.

Building

What building it taught me

Designing and building the same product meant the hard parts showed up on the device, not in the file. A few that taught me the most:

The detent saga

Getting pop-ups to hug their content through the system’s sheet detents failed four different ways, ending in a mystery dark rectangle that turned out to be the sheet’s own shadow. The fix was to stop using sheets: pop-ups became overlays inside the view, exactly as tall as what they hold, with the nav still visible behind a light scrim. When a system component fights the design four times, stop negotiating with it.

Masked builds

Three “fixes” in a row never reached the phone. Piping a build through tail reports the pipe’s exit code, so a failed compile looked like a success and the old binary kept getting reinstalled. The fix was set -o pipefail and an explicit build-ok gate before any install. The lesson stuck: verify the tool’s own exit status, never the pipeline’s.

The quiet delete

Deleting your account looked like it worked but left your data on the server. The app had wrapped the delete in a silent try? and logged out regardless, faking success, while a platform rule change had broken the delete itself. Now the app removes your photos through the Storage API, deletes the account, and shows an error if anything fails. Never hide a failure the user needs to know about.

Enter the app

The open-the-app animation never felt right through pass after pass of tuning. The real problem was structural: in the prototype the map is alive under onboarding and entering just fades a veil off it, but the Swift app was building the map at the moment of reveal, so there was nothing behind the curtain. Mounting the app as the floor fixed it. Mechanism over easing curves.

Shipping

Where it is now

Booth is on the App Store submission track. The backend is live, with row-level security on every table, nearly 400 booths seeded and verified across 11 cities, and passwordless sign-in running on Twilio Verify. Builds 3 through 7 are on TestFlight with the full listing, privacy, and support pages in place. The one call left before review is moving off the free database tier, so the project cannot pause itself mid-review. After that, it is wider city coverage and getting Booth into real hands.

Learnings

What I’d take forward

Designing and building the same thing changes both.

The device kept the design honest. A map that looked finished in the file was being born at the moment of reveal; taps that read fine on screen were dead on glass. Shipping the code is what caught the gap between the design and what people actually get.

One source of truth beats two good ones.

Making the prototype law, and porting to it rather than around it, meant the app never quietly drifted. Every change landed in both surfaces or neither. That rule did more for consistency than any amount of after-the-fact cleanup.

Honesty is a feature.

“Not here yet” instead of an empty map. A photo required before a booth goes live. A failed delete that actually says so. Each one costs a shortcut and buys the thing a small app lives or dies on: trust.
